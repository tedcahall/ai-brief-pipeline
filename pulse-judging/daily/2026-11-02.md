## Judge notes (labels refer to each judge's own blind shuffle)

**opus:** L won on hierarchy and throughline: "Anthropic's amended S-1 put real numbers behind the AI buildout," followed by "OpenAI answered on the product side" and "Governments are moving too," which gives the reader a narrative rather than a list, though its tail crams in DeepSeek and GDPx. I was close behind with an integrating lead ("Frontier-agent evaluation and orchestration moved forward") and a why-line ("a concrete example of criminals engineering around model refusals"). K lost: it is a bare list with an empty item ("a new Nvidia benchmark submission reported faster inference on large models") and an 'also' tack-on about Karpathy. B and C also fell low, B for the garbled "switches providers when generating victim assessments is refused" and C for repeated disclaimers like "should not be mistaken for observed containment failures."

**glm:** K won because it builds narratives rather than lists — 'Agent architecture is now the axis Chinese labs are competing on rather than parameter counts' gives each cluster a reason to matter, and its one caveat ('a vendor-supplied figure that has not been independently validated') is integrated rather than repeated. G ran second on a strong financial lead ('Anthropic's amended S-1 put real numbers behind the AI buildout') but stumbled with a three-clause run-on final sentence that crams Korea, DeepSeek and GDPx together. D lost: beyond thin one-line items, 'a new Nvidia benchmark submission reported faster inference on large models' is too vague to inform, and A's double disclaimer ('should not be mistaken for observed containment failures') capped an otherwise listy summary.

**astra:** J wins narrowly because "targeting the human attention bottleneck in parallel agent work" supplies an executive-relevant interpretation, and its malware ending explains a mechanism rather than merely naming a release. It still has multiple flaws and is not publication-ready. A loses because "highlighted the cost line for Carbon" conveys almost no useful information, while "HBM past 1,000 tok/s per user on 700B+ models" exemplifies its severe specification overload and scattered hierarchy.

**grok:** The best won by naming a throughline and interpreting the figure: "GDPx leaves substantial headroom, with Claude Opus 5.5 leading its debut leaderboard at 12%, while DeepSeek is targeting the human attention bottleneck in parallel agent work." A reader finishes knowing what the day was about; the unattached inference sentence is why it is a 4 and not a 5. The worst lost on a sentence that does not parse — "malware that switches providers when generating victim assessments is refused" — and on a thin list (a forthcoming repo, a quantization mention, a math aside) that never says why any item matters.

**dsflash:** The winner (B) is the only entry that ends a block with an actual argument: VOLTA is "a concrete example of model routing used to bypass guardrails in real-world cyberattacks" — the reader learns something about the shape of the threat, not just that it exists. It still pays for density ("ahead of Gemini 4 Carbon at 10.9% and GPT-6 Astra at 9.6%") and one Bloomberg/Next Web double-attribution run-on, which keeps it off a 4. Runner-up F wins on hierarchy — the S-1 numbers "put real numbers behind the AI buildout" before pivoting to OpenAI's product answer and then to governments — but blunts it with a 45-word obligations sentence and a vague "four Abu Dhabi agreements spanning government, energy and investment." The worst (G) fails at the level of information: "a new Nvidia benchmark submission reported faster inference on large models" could describe any quarter, and the item closes on a tacked-on Karpathy aside ("Andrej Karpathy also showed how he used Claude") that belongs in a newsletter, not a brief. D is nearly as weak for the opposite reason — it spends its limited space on negations ("should not be mistaken for observed containment failures") instead of findings. C has the day's best analytical instinct ("Agent architecture is now the axis Chinese labs are competing on rather than parameter counts") but 286 words, four run-ons and a stack of model names make it a homework assignment.
