**glm:** M wins: it opens with the day's real story ('Nvidia's board met Wednesday to decide whether to remove CEO Jensen Huang'), adds reporting texture (successor search, interim CEO talk) and closes with a concrete benchmark result; its only sins are density in the final clause. F is close behind with the same strong lead and a disciplined model roundup, only slightly overloaded. J loses: at 60 words it is a list of vague fragments ('with availability still limited') that orients no one. G also fails: two hedging disclaimers plus a closing line the reader must decode ('why failed attacks mean less when agents barely test the intended boundary'). Most mid-pack entries drowned the same five launches in parameter stacks (280B total / 14B active appears in half the field) and while-joined item chains instead of a throughline.

**astra:** D wins because it establishes a consequential lead immediately—"Nvidia's board met Wednesday to decide whether to remove CEO Jensen Huang"—and gives readers an understandable market consequence before moving to secondary developments. Its benchmark-heavy ending keeps it below publication-ready perfection. G finishes last because almost every sentence asks the reader to unpack another technical inventory: "a post-trained 280-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with 14 billion active parameters" is emblematic. The umbrella phrase "Across agentic systems and governance" does not create a real connection between the crowded items.

**grok:** D won by giving the day a scene and a stake before the catalog starts: "hours after The New York Times detailed a second alleged affair with an Nvidia vice president who answers to him," then the share drop and a search for successors, including a possible interim CEO. The release graf is only a name list, but the reader already knows what mattered. B lost because the connectors do all the work and the claims do not land: "OpenAI has set a transition date" never gives the date, and "failed attacks mean less when agents barely test the intended boundary" spends its one interpretive sentence on a hedge.

**dsflash:** L won because it alone built a hierarchy and said why the lead matters — "leaving the industry's most important chip supplier without an obvious successor" — then kept the release round in a clearly subordinate position. B, M and O are close behind on the same Nvidia lead but dilute it: B with "Wednesday was also a heavy release day", M with a 45-word attribution stack, O with an unconfirmed rumor ("neither company has confirmed that"). The 3s (C, F, P, R, H) are accurate lists without a spine; H is the clearest case, publishing that Perplexity "published results from red-teaming 12 computer agents" and never saying what they showed. E lost outright: three vague items, one of them "with availability still limited", and an NPR swatting item attached by "Meanwhile".
