M finishes last: it hedges emptily ('should be understood in the context of their specific tests'), leaves its items vague ('an inference accelerator', 'a research credit program'), and gives the reader no hierarchy.

**glm:** G wins by telling each story fully with concrete stakes — the Riemann compression opens with who, what and independent verification ('shrinks Levent Alpöge's 17.4-million-line formalization to about 76,000 lines, and two outside mathematicians have independently checked it'), and its Japan close names the actual duty. W loses: its centerpiece benchmark sentence collapses under its own numbers ('showed up to 2.28x better performance per dollar than GB300 NVL72 on a GLM model, which SemiAnalysis calls an official preview' — the reader cannot tell what was tested or claimed), and it fuses unrelated policy items with 'and also'. G's only sin is an attribution stack; W's is structural.

**astra:** U wins for readable distinctions and a useful organizing sentence, "Two programs lower the cost of putting models to work"; it translates technical results into understandable use cases. D loses because "AA-Image-T2I v2.1 leaderboard, while Tencent Hunyuan" exemplifies its overloaded naming, and "benchmarks evaluating" reports activity instead of findings. No entry merits a 5.

**grok:** I won because it opens with a claim an executive can use, "Custom cloud silicon challenged Nvidia's inference moat," then qualifies the leaderboard and names the risk instead of stacking releases. **dsflash:** J wins on hierarchy: it opens with a thesis ("Custom cloud silicon challenged Nvidia's inference moat") rather than a product, then explains the qualifier that matters ("its lead relies heavily on alignment tasks while trailing Claude Fable 5.2 in software engineering"), so the reader leaves knowing what changed.
